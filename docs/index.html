How it works ↓

The dashboard problem

"We asked our team: when was the last time a dashboard helped you fix an incident?"

The answer was silence. Everyone looked at logs.

We had built dashboards for every new service - Prometheus, Grafana, the whole stack. Years later, not a single engineer on the team used them during an outage. They grepped logs, checked process state, traced what happened around the error. The dashboards were there because "that is what monitoring looks like," not because they solved problems.

microSRE is built for people who already know this. Alerts when something is wrong. The tools you already use to investigate. No graphs to scroll, no panels to configure, no compliance theater.

How it works

microSRE reads /proc directly - the same kernel interface that ps, top, and iostat use, but without the fragile parsing and version drift. It samples CPU, memory, disk I/O, and network counters, then diffs them to produce rates.

What "needs you" means: for every resource - CPU, memory, disk, network - microSRE reports utilization, saturation, and errors, the USE-method taxonomy, from whatever the kernel exposes. CPU: utilization and run queue. Memory: utilization, pressure-stall averages, and OOM kills. Disk: utilization, await, and queue depth. Network: throughput, TCP retransmits, and listen-backlog overflows. Thresholds are yours to set; the signals are the kernel's.

It also watches application logs you specify, matching each line against error patterns. When error volume crosses your threshold in a time window, it alerts. No SDK to install, no code to instrument, no vendor to depend on. And it measures itself: every tick reports how long its own collection took (collect.*_seconds), so its cost is checkable on your machine, not promised in a README.

Alerts go to Slack, Discord, or Telegram. That channel is the record. No dashboards. No data-source sprawl. No lock-in. Your metrics, your logs, your machine.

No root required. Runs as a regular user out of the box. Root (or the docker group) adds container log collection, and system journal access widens OOM-kill detection - useful, not required.

Why /proc directly

Standard tools are built for human eyes, not automation. Each one re-scans the same kernel counters, reformats them for a terminal, and truncates whatever didn't fit a readable column. Reading /proc directly means one scan instead of many, trivial parsing instead of format-string archaeology, and the full field - nothing lost in translation to a human-friendly layout.

Reading /proc directly gives you:

  • Stability - kernel ABI, not userland format strings
  • Speed - no fork/exec, no parsing overhead
  • Portability - works in minimal containers, rescue shells, locked-down hosts
  • Completeness - access fields that ps and top hide or misrepresent
  • Early warning - stay ahead of the OOM killer by monitoring /proc/pressure/memory, the same pressure signal systemd-oomd watches when it decides which process to kill

To be fair, /proc drifts too: fields appear and change meaning across kernel versions - PSI itself only landed in 4.20, and some counters mean different things on different filesystems. So every signal microSRE reports is traced to the exact file and field it comes from, in the documentation. No counter is named on faith.

Why logs directly

Log scanning extends the same philosophy: observe applications from the outside, without modifying them. Your logs already contain the errors; you just have to alert on them. No code changes - just patterns and thresholds. Your logs, your choice: keep them local, archive them, or ship them to centralized storage. microSRE alerts you when bad things happen; the rest is up to you. No SDK to break, no network dependency to fail, no vendor to disappear.

It's the observer that can't be blinded by the observed.

No hoarding

Most monitoring agents run a hoard-first pipeline: buffer every metric and every log line "just in case", then forward all of it to a remote store. microSRE does the opposite - it reads the source in place, acts on it, and keeps nothing beyond the current check. What to keep and for how long - logs local, archived, shipped, or sampled away - is your decision, not the agent's. (Traces are a different story: they answer questions metrics and logs structurally cannot, and shipping those is a reasonable choice. It is the ship-everything-by-default culture around metrics and logs we opt out of.)

Notifications get the same treatment. On-call tools keep their own log of every page, acknowledgment, and escalation - a second incident history, stored inside the vendor's system. For a large org that audit trail earns its keep. But the alert already landed in your chat, the discussion happened there, and that history is searchable, backed up, and yours. Replaying last night's pages should not require logging into another tool. Where a formal trail is genuinely needed, it should be an explicit opt-in - like shipping logs or adding dashboards - not a side effect of being paged.

Across your fleet

Built for fleets of pets, not herds of cattle - the tens of servers you know by name, not thousands you don't.

Running a fleet means deciding across machines, not staring at one. The hosted view is your command and control center: the same 60-second health check for every server, every signal in one place - alerts and next actions based on the whole picture, not a single metric in isolation. A ward monitor for your infrastructure - every server's vitals at a glance, anomalies raised - not another dashboard builder. With a longer history and advanced alerting rules. Logs never leave your machines, and no continuous metric stream does either; the hosted view receives only the periodic health summary and alert state.

Private beta includes:

  • Dead man's switch - If a machine stops checking in, you know. No heartbeat means an alert, not a missing data point.
  • Only wake up for fires - Critical alerts page you immediately. Everything else batches into a digest or waits for business hours.
  • One digest, not ten pings - Medium alerts collect into a single timed summary with context and a suggested command. Route critical alerts to #incidents, digests to #alerts silently.
  • Escalation without babysitting - If a medium alert persists, it automatically upgrades to critical after a timer you set. Historic baselines help tune thresholds.
